5  Elevation, slope, and wind speed

Dates of practical: 6th and 13th November 2025

Elevation is a key determinant of many geographical processes. The height of the land determines whether the coast is above or below sea level. Mountain and valley slopes determine water flow and microclimates. Biomes are often restricted to particular altitudes. Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) describe land surface height in a grid, and are a special case of continuous geospatial data widely available as raster datasets at various spatial resolutions. QGIS has several tools to inspect and manipulate DEMs, which are necessary for the wind farm project.

This exercise will guide you through the requirements for making Map 3

NoteLearning aims

To visualise DEMs and wind speed data, subset, classify and vectorize, and create slope and aspect images. These steps are vital for the wind farm project.

5.1 Adding rasters

  1. Launch QGIS and create a new project

  2. From the Wales_DEM folder, add the SRTM_Wales_DEM_25m.tif layer to your project. This DEM is derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission, which scanned nearly 80% of Earth’s land surfaces in 2000 to generate a DEM at 25 m resolution.

  3. From the layer’s symbology (Properties... → Symbology), experiment with the Singleband pseudocolor render type (see Section 2.5)

  4. Click the drop-down arrow to the right of the Color ramp

  5. Click Create New Color Ramp

  1. Under Please select color ramp type:, select Catalog: cpt‑city. This ‘city’ of Colour Palette Tables (CPTs) is a repository of colour tables designed to creatively present raster data

  2. Explore the Topography theme

  3. Experiment with adjusting:

    1. The Color ramp
    2. Min / max under the Min / Max Value Settings drop-down menu iii.Mode (Continuous, Equal Interval, and Quantile)

  4. Click OK

5.2 Inspecting rasters

  1. Install the Value Tool

  2. Click the Value Tool icon (). This tool allows you to interrogate data values as you pass the cursor over rasters

  3. Find the heights of Yr Wyddfa (Eryri) and Pen y Fan (Bannau Brycheiniog). Check your heights against those on Google

  4. Add the Wales_operational_windfarms.gpkg layer to your project

  5. Reflect on where these wind farms are located. How do their locations correlate with elevation?

5.3 Clipping rasters

  1. Clip the DEM to your map tile:

    1. Click Raster → Extraction → Clip Raster by Mask Layer...
    2. Set Input layer to SRTM_Wales_DEM_25m
    3. SetMask layer to your map tile. You can access layers even if they aren’t loaded in your project using the Browse icon ()
    4. Click the Browse icon () next to Clipped (mask) and enter a sensible file name (e.g. SH22_DEM) and location on your OneDrive account to save the clipped raster
    5. Click Save → Run → Close
    6. Have you saved your project recently?

  2. Explore your map tile’s DEM using a suitable colour palette and different Min / max ranges

  3. Identify the highest point in your clipped DEM and, if possible, its name by Googling

  4. Here’s an opportunity to keep your project clutter-free. Remove the SRTM_Wales_DEM_25m and Wales_operational_windfarms layers from your project

5.4 Viewing maps in 3D

  1. View the clipped DEM in 3D:

    1. Click View → 3D Map Views → New 3D Map View
    2. Click the Configure... icon ()
    3. Click Terrain
    4. Set Type to DEM (Raster Layer)
    5. Set Elevation to your clipped DEM
    6. Set Vertical scale to 4
    7. Set Tile resolution to 64 px
    8. Click OK
    9. Use the mouse (by scrolling and holding down the click on the scroller) to explore the 3D map
    10. Experiment with different values of Vertical scale and Tile resolution
    11. Add other layers to see them plotted in 3D

5.4.1 Deriving a slope raster

Slope (gradient of the land surface) is fundamental properties of the land surface topography and can enlighten many geophysical processes such erosion.

  1. Derive a slope raster from a DEM:

    1. Click Raster → Analysis → Slope...
    2. Set Input layer as your clipped DEM
    3. Click the Browse icon () next to Slope to save the slope raster somewhere sensible
    4. Click Save → Run → Close
    5. Use the layer’s legend to identify the steepest areas. Why are they there?

5.5 Deriving an aspect raster

Aspect (compass direction towards which the gradient faces) is fundamental properties of the land surface topography and can enlighten many geophysical processes such as insolation.

  1. Derive an aspect raster from a DEM:

    1. Click Raster → Analysis → Aspect...
    2. Set Input layer as your clipped DEM
    3. Click the Browse icon () next to Aspect to save the aspect raster somewhere sensible
    4. Click Save → Run → Close An aspect of 1° (just East of North) is very close in meaning to an aspect of 359° (just West of North), yet they are at the opposite ends of the decimal geographic coordinate numerical range. A colour palette table with the same colour for both ends of the value range would make it easier to interpret your aspect layer
    5. Navigate to the cpt‑city colour Palette Tables (CPTs) (see Section 5.1)
    6. Click the All by author tab
    7. In the Name window, click grass
    8. In the GRASS colour ramps window, click aspect Click OK → OK

5.6 Deriving a hillshade raster

Hill-shading simulates the effect of light falling on a surface by combining slope, aspect and a solar illumination direction. This approach is commonly used to visualize topography in maps and figures.

  1. Derive a hillshade raster from a DEM:

    1. Click Raster → Analysis → Hillshade...
    2. Set Input layer as your clipped DEM
    3. Click the Browse icon () next to Hillshade to save the aspect raster somewhere sensible
    4. Click Save → Run → Close
    5. From the Layers panel, turn off layer visibilities or arrange layers so that your hillshade layer is above your DEM layer
    6. Change the hillshade transparency to 50%
    7. Change the symology of your DEM to something intuitive for land height (e.g. Greens)

    Experiment with Z factor (probably no more than 5) Experiment with Azimuth of the light Experiment with Altitude of the light

This is a rare example where making a layer semi-transparent can be useful. The effect is often used to make maps visually engaging

5.7 Classifying raster data

Use the raster calculator to perform cell-by-cell operations, such as to find parts of a raster layer within a specified range of values. One of the constraints for building a windfarm is that the land surface slope should be no more than 6 degrees. This is simply because greater slopes are less stable and not normally viable to build on. Build an expression in the raster calculator as follows:

i. Click `Raster` → `Raster Calculator...` 
ii. Under `Raster Bands`, double-click on the slope raster name you created earlier
iii. In the `Raster Calculation Expression` window, add `<6`
iv. Click the `Browse` icon (![](figs/Browse.png){width=3%}) next to `Output layer` to save the classified slope raster somewhere sensible
v. Click `OK`

The output raster has two values; `0` (black) and `1` (white). Given you instructed the raster calculator to identify areas of the raster that are less than 6°, what do you think these colours represent? If one can build on a slope only on land less than 6°, which colour represents suitable areas to bulid wind farms?

5.8 Converting to vector polygons

Vector polygons can be created from raster layers so that they can be combined with other vector constraints such as those created in chapter 4.

5.8.0.1 Polygonize

  1. Convert raster classifications to vector polygons:

    1. Click Raster → Conversion → Polygonize (Raster to Vector)...
    2. Set Input layer as your classified slope layer
    3. Set Name of the field to create as Suitable
    4. Click Run → Close

  2. Polygons now demarcate the areas that are suitable (slope < 6°) or unsuitable (slope > 6°) for building upon. Open the attribute table to explore which is which. Think binary; under the ‘Suitable’ column, 1s represent ‘yes’, 0s represent ‘no’

  3. Visualise the suitable and unsuitable areas for construction in the symbology (see Section 1.6.0.1)

  4. Select the ‘unsuitable’ polygons (Suitable = 0) using Select features using an expression (see Section 4.8)

  5. Export the selected polygons (remember to use the Save Selected Features As... option). Name and store sensibly

5.9 Wind speeds

  1. From the Wales_mean_windspeed folder, add the Wales_wind-speed_50m.tif layer to your project

  2. Read over the description_and_data_source.txt file in the Wales_mean_windspeed folder for details about the data. Note; description_and_data_source.txt files appear with every dataset you use

  3. Clip the wind speed raster to your tile and save it somewhere sensible (see Section 5.3)

  4. Visualise the data using a suitable colour palette

5.10 Putting it all together

  1. Reopen your constraint layers project from Exercise 4, and add your polygonised slope constraint

  2. With the wind speed layer beneath constraints, explore where development may be permitted and optimal. you can explore not just where building a windfarm may be permitted, but where the windiest (best power generation) and largest (most number of turbines possible) plots of land are located in your map tile area

  3. Design Map 3 of your wind farm Project to clearly show the best otherwise‑unconstrained wind farm location, and use the various tools (e.g raster calculator) to extract information to help you justify why

TipLearning outcomes

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  1. Visualise raster elevation data using pseudocolour ramps, cpt-city palettes, and min/max scaling, and interrogate cell values directly with the Value Tool.
  2. Clip raster layers to an area of interest and explore the resulting surface in three dimensions.
  3. Derive slope, aspect, and hillshade rasters from a DEM, and explain what each reveals about surface topography and geophysical process.
  4. Classify raster data with the raster calculator to identify areas meeting a defined threshold, and convert the result to vector polygons for combination with other constraint layers.
  5. Appreciate how elevation, slope, and wind speed interact to determine site suitability, integrating raster-derived constraints into a justified development decision.
